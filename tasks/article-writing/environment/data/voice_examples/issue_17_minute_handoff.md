# The 17-minute handoff

*A cleaner close is worth more than another dashboard.*

At 5:43 p.m., Westline’s evening lead stopped rebuilding the day from chat messages.

That was the visible result of a four-week test of Shift Notes. The less visible result mattered more: the morning and evening leads finally argued over the same record. No screenshot archaeology. No “I thought that was yours.” No third version of the close living in somebody’s notebook.

This is a small product story. It is also a useful operations story, because handoffs fail in small ways long before they fail in expensive ones.

## Start with what changed hands

Westline runs appliance repair from five branches. Before the test, each branch closed differently. One lead posted a paragraph in chat. Another shared a sheet. A third called the person taking over and hoped they wrote things down.

The information was familiar: jobs still open, customers waiting on a call, technicians carrying parts home, and work that had to move before the first route went out. The format was the problem.

Shift Notes gives the closing lead one short queue tied to the day’s work. An open job can be handed to the next shift with an owner, a reason, and a next action. The receiving lead marks it seen. Nothing here is trying to run the branch. It is trying to make the branch legible.

Westline measured the time between the evening lead starting the close and the morning lead acknowledging the handoff. The median fell from 31 minutes to 14. The team also counted how often a morning lead had to ask for missing context. That count fell from 46 requests in the first week to 19 in the fourth.

Those numbers do not prove that every branch will cut its close in half. They do show where the time went: not into decisions, but into finding the state of the work.

## Make the exception carry its own context

The strongest note in the test was not the longest. It read: “WO-1847 — compressor on van 12; Sam owns the 8 a.m. customer call; hold route slot until 8:15.”

That note has a job number, a physical fact, an owner, and a deadline. A receiving lead can act without asking the sender to reconstruct the evening.

Most handoff software gets worse when it becomes a second work-management system. People copy every update into it, the queue grows, and the useful exceptions disappear under routine status. Westline set a narrower rule: hand off only work that could change the next shift’s first hour.

That rule did more than the template. By week four, the average note was shorter, while acknowledgments got faster.

## Keep a person on the hook

Shift Notes does not assign work on its own. That is deliberate. A branch lead still decides who owns the next move, because technician availability, customer history, and local judgment do not collapse cleanly into a status field.

The product makes one promise: when that decision is made, the next person can see it beside the work it affects.

For Westline, the remaining problem is weekend coverage. The test included only weekday closes, and two branches rotate weekend leads differently. We will not turn a weekday result into a seven-day claim.

Before you add another close-of-day meeting, take the last ten questions your opening lead asked. If the answer existed but could not be found, bring those ten misses to a workflow review. That is the handoff worth fixing.
